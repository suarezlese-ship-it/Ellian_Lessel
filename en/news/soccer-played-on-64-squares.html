Soccer Played on 64 Squares

We all say soccer is like chess. Few of us ask what would happen if it really were. El tablero de césped turns that phrase into a method.

By Ellian Lessel·Published November 15, 2026·3 min read

Few comparisons are repeated as often in soccer as this one: “the match was a game of chess.” Commentators say it when play gets bogged down, coaches when they win on tactics, and fans when they cannot explain why their team never reached the goal. It is a comfortable phrase. It suggests intelligence, patience, calculation. But it is almost never examined.

El tablero de césped (“The Turf Board”; Lessel, 2026) begins exactly there, at the point where the phrase runs out. Its proposal is to take it seriously: if soccer is chess, it must be possible to describe it with the same precision as a game of chess.

A pitch with coordinates

The first step is geometric. The book lays over the pitch a board of 64 squares, each 13 by 9 meters. Suddenly, soccer’s vague expressions become exact. “Through the middle” becomes D4. “Near the box” becomes F6. A diagonal stops being a hunch and becomes a line that can be drawn.

This is no whim. In chess, notation makes it possible to record, analyze, and compare games played a century ago. A shared language for the space of soccer would allow something similar: talking about the game with fewer adjectives and more coordinates.

Pieces that change their nature

But soccer is not chess, and the book knows it. On the board, a bishop is always a bishop: it moves diagonally from the first move to the last. On the grass, by contrast, the same player changes function several times a minute.

The book calls this phenomenon transmutation, and it is the central idea of the whole system. A center-back can be a rook when holding the line and an advanced pawn when stepping forward with the ball. A winger can work as a bishop hugging the touchline and, a second later, as a queen slipping between the lines. Reading a match, from this point of view, means reading those transformations.

Zugzwang and pressing

Chess players have a German word for an uncomfortable situation: zugzwang. It is the moment when a player is forced to move, and any move worsens their position. They would like to pass, but cannot.

Soccer has its own zugzwang, though it does not call it that. When a team presses well, closes every passing lane, and leaves the opponent with the ball and no good options, that opponent is forced to do something that hurts them: a back pass, a hopeful long ball, a turnover in a dangerous area. The book devotes pages to these situations, along with the pin, the fork, and castling, and translates them into scenes recognizable in any match.

From kickoff to penalties

Like a game of chess, a match has phases: an opening, a middlegame, an endgame. The book goes through them one by one, all the way to the penalty shootout, which in this light looks very much like a chess endgame with few pieces left, where every decision weighs double. It also analyzes three great philosophies of play: the Soviet school of scientific soccer, the counterattack, and gegenpressing, the immediate press after losing the ball.

A language, not a recipe

Anyone looking for a formula for winning will not find one. What the book offers is something else: a vocabulary. And a good vocabulary changes the way we see. After reading it, it is hard to watch a match without asking which square the play is on, which piece each player is at that instant, and who, without knowing it, is in zugzwang.

It is meant for coaches who sense patterns and want to name them, for analysts looking for a system rather than a collection of slogans, and for chess players who watch a match and suspect there is another game underneath.

El tablero de césped is written and published in Spanish.
