Behind Ad Primordia

What If Myths Were Memory?

Floods, gods descending from the sky, and wise figures who warn humanity: the great traditions of ancient Mesopotamia share too many pieces. Ad Primordia begins from that suspicion.

By Ellian Lessel·Published November 15, 2026·3 min read

Around the middle of the nineteenth century, in the ruins of Nineveh, archaeologists brought to light thousands of clay tablets covered in wedge-shaped signs (Layard, 1853). Years later, when they could finally be read, one of them caused a sensation in London (Smith, 1873): it told of a flood sent by the gods, a man warned in time, a vessel loaded with life, and a bird released to look for dry land. It was a Babylonian story, far older than the texts Europe knew.

Ever since, we have known something that unsettles any attentive reader: the great ancient traditions resemble one another too closely.

One story, many voices

The flood appears in the Sumerian tradition with Ziusudra (Kramer, 1961), in the Akkadian with Atrahasis (Lambert & Millard, 1969), in the Epic of Gilgamesh with Utnapishtim (George, 2003), and in the Bible with Noah (Genesis 6–9). In almost every version, a wise figure warns of the disaster. In the Mesopotamian account, that role falls to Enki, god of fresh water and of knowledge, who finds a way to alert a man without openly defying the other gods (Lambert & Millard, 1969).

There are other echoes. The garden of Dilmun, the pure land of Sumerian myth where neither sickness nor old age existed (Kramer, 1961), recalls other lost paradises. The texts speak of beings who descend from the sky, of knowledge handed down to humankind, of an age before our own. Later, the Book of Enoch would tell the story of the Watchers, who come down to earth and teach forbidden arts (Nickelsburg, 2001).

Historians explain many of these coincidences through cultural transmission: peoples conquered one another, traded, copied, and rewrote their neighbors’ stories. It is a solid explanation. But literature has the privilege of asking what if.

The novel’s question

Ad Primordia (Lessel, 2026) starts from a narrative hypothesis: what if each ancient people had kept a different fragment of the same memory, and mistaken it for the origin of everything? What if myths were not mere inventions, but memories distorted by time, translation, and power?

The novel does not put this idea forward as historical theory, but as the engine of fiction. And it sets that engine running in two timelines. In 2034, an excavation no one wanted to fund at Tell Al-Madiné, on the lower Euphrates, uncovers a six-sided temple and the Ark of Knowledge, a set of tablets that are not made of clay. Archaeologist Sarah Alcázar and her team begin to decipher them. In the other, far older thread of the story, a civilization called the Akhuri witnesses the death throes of its world and prepares to cross the void.

Between the two threads runs a voice: the logbook of Enki. The name is no accident, and anyone familiar with Sumerian mythology will recognize several of the characters. The novel rereads them freely, without claiming that its rereading is the truth of history.

Rigor in fiction

The saga belongs to hard science fiction: the physics of travel, the length of the voyages, and the survival of a population in deep space are treated with respect for what science allows. The same rigor applies to the ancient sources. The myths that appear in the novel exist; the names have roots; the landscapes of the Euphrates are real. The invention lies in the bridges built between them.

This first volume is titled Despertar (“Awakening”), and the title is literal. It does not reveal everything the saga holds, nor does it try to. It is the moment when a buried voice finally finds someone willing to listen. What that voice has to say will unfold over the course of five volumes.

Reading with new eyes

Perhaps the best proof that a myth is still alive is that it still asks us questions. The next time you read the flood story, in any of its versions, pay attention to the figure who warns of the disaster. Ask yourself what that figure knew, how it came to know it, and what it cost to say it.

Ad Primordia is written and published in Spanish.
