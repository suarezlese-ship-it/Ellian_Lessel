What Is a Civilization Worth?

Since 1964 we have measured civilizations by the energy they control. What if the true measure were efficiency? A question that also brushes against the great silence of the universe.

By Ellian Lessel·Published November 15, 2026·3 min read

In 1964, the Soviet astrophysicist Nikolai Kardashev proposed a way to imagine civilizations more advanced than ours. His criterion was energy. A Type I civilization would harness power comparable to that of its whole planet; a Type II, that of its star; a Type III, that of its entire galaxy (Kardashev, 1964). Years later, Carl Sagan turned that scale into a continuous formula and calculated that humanity has not yet reached Type I: we would stand at roughly 0.7 (Sagan, 1973).

The Kardashev scale is elegant and easy to remember. That is why it became famous. But it carries an assumption that is rarely discussed: that advancing means, above all, consuming more.

The brute-force trap

La eficiencia espaciotemporal (“Spacetime Efficiency”; Lessel, 2026) starts from a simple objection. If a civilization multiplied its energy use by burning more fuel, the classic scale would see it progress. Yet it might be destroying its biosphere and moving toward collapse. Measuring power alone is like rating a chess player by how many pieces they move rather than by the quality of their play.

The book proposes changing the question. Not how much energy a civilization controls, but how efficiently it uses it: how much information it processes, what distances it can cover without fragmenting, how much entropy it generates for each unit of useful work.

Six variables and an equation

From that idea comes the Spacetime Efficiency Index, represented by the Greek letter Φ. It is calculated with a Master Equation that combines six variables: energy scale, technological maturity, cognitive capacity, the distance a civilization commands, the latency of its information, and its entropic efficiency. Each is bounded by a known physical law, from Einstein’s relativity to the limits of computation.

The equation has an interesting property: it penalizes imbalance. If any one of the basic capacities falls to zero, the whole index collapses. No civilization can make up with power what it lacks in intelligence or in care for its environment.

Using 2025 data, the diagnosis for humanity is sobering: Φ ≈ 0.002. We are at Grade 0 on a scale that runs to 5. And the analysis reveals something unexpected: the most effective lever for advancing is not producing more energy, but wasting less.

The great silence

There is a question that runs through the whole book, one Enrico Fermi is said to have posed over lunch in 1950 (Jones, 1985): if the universe is so vast and so old, where is everybody? The Drake equation (Drake, 1965) tried to estimate how many civilizations might be able to communicate with us, and one of its terms, the lifetime of those civilizations, remains the most uncertain.

The search goes on. In 1977, a radio telescope in Ohio recorded a signal so strong and so unexpected that an astronomer wrote “Wow!” in the margin of the printout (Ehman, 1998). It was never repeated, and its origin was never confirmed. The book does not present it as proof of anything, but as a reminder that the silence is not yet settled.

The book’s theory suggests one possible answer among others: perhaps the most advanced civilizations are not the loudest. A highly efficient civilization would waste little energy and would emit its waste heat at very low temperatures, almost indistinguishable from the background of the cosmos. The book calls this idea the Principle of Elegant Invisibility. If it were true, we would be surrounded not by silence, but by discretion.

A theory to be challenged

The author presents his proposal honestly: it is an open theory, not yet peer-reviewed, that includes predictions capable of refuting it. He invites readers to read with a pencil in hand, to redo the calculations, and to look for the variable that is missing or the one that does not belong.

It is not a book for those who want certainties. It is for those who suspect that the big questions deserve more than speculation.

La eficiencia espaciotemporal is written and published in Spanish.
